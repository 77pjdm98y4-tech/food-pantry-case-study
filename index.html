The problem

Paper logs and spreadsheets couldn't keep up

A food pantry runs on three loops at once. Donations come in and have to be logged and shelved. Households come in and need a fair share of what's on the shelves. Volunteers have to be scheduled so there are enough hands on distribution days.

Each loop was tracked separately, by different people. Staff couldn't see stock levels while handing out food. Coordinators couldn't see who was available when building a shift. Nobody could easily tell whether a household had already been served that month.

The goal: one system where each person sees exactly the part of the work they do, with the rules of the pantry built in so the right choice is the easy one.

Research

What interviews and task analysis showed

I interviewed pantry staff and volunteer coordinators and broke each recurring job into steps: donation intake, inventory checks, household registration, distribution, and shift scheduling. I looked for where people paused, double-checked, or had to ask someone else.

Staff workflows

Too much on screen slows people down

Staff and coordinators do different jobs. Showing everyone every menu made the tool harder to learn, so access needed to follow the role.

Volunteers

Clear schedules reduce no-shows

Coordinators needed to see each volunteer's availability while assigning shifts, not in a separate list.

Households

Fairness depends on frequency

Without a record of the last visit, some households were served twice in a month while others missed out.

Inventory

Stockouts are found too late

Low stock was usually discovered at the shelf during distribution. Staff needed alerts before that point.

Design principles

Four rules that shaped every screen

Prevent errors at the input

Required fields, number limits tied to real stock, and confirmations before anything is deleted.

Show only what the role needs

Navigation is built per role, so a coordinator never wades through inventory screens.

Give feedback in real time

Status pills, live totals and color-coded counts let people decide at a glance.

Match the real task order

Screens follow the sequence people already use at the pantry, from left to right and top to bottom.

Key workflows

Where the design does the work

FLOW 01 · DISTRIBUTION

Fair allocation, with a guardrail instead of a wall

  • Eligibility is checked automatically. If a household received food within the pantry's window (30 days by default), a warning appears at the top with the exact date.
  • Staff can still say yes. Emergencies happen, so the rule can be overridden with a checkbox. The override is saved with the record so it can be reviewed later.
  • The system suggests a quantity based on household size, so staff start from a fair number instead of guessing.
  • Stock limits the inputs. Each field's maximum is the amount on hand, and items at zero are disabled, so no one can hand out food the pantry doesn't have.

FLOW 02 · DONATION INTAKE

Logging a mixed donation in one pass

  • Rows match how donations arrive: a box of mixed items. Staff add or remove rows instead of submitting one item at a time.
  • The unit fills in automatically when an item is chosen (cans, lbs, dozens), which removes a common source of mismatched counts.
  • A live total lets staff check the count against the box before saving.
  • A first-time donor is one click away with an Add New Donor button right under the donor list.

Testing & iteration

Watching people use it, then changing it

I observed staff and coordinators completing real tasks on the prototype and reviewed usage data: abandoned forms, login errors, distribution overrides and inventory edits. Each finding led to a specific change.

What I sawWhat changed

Staff entered quantities with typos and negative numbers during donation intake.

Numeric fields got minimums, step sizes and stock-based maximums, and empty rows are ignored on save.

Coordinators kept switching screens to check who was free before assigning a shift.

The assignment screen now lists each volunteer's available days beside the checkbox.

Household lookup failed when staff only had a phone number or ZIP code.

Search now matches last name, household ID or ZIP, with an active/inactive filter.

How it's built

A data model shaped by the workflows

I designed the database after mapping the tasks, so each table matches something staff actually handle: a receipt, a line item, a shift. The app runs on Python's standard library with SQLite, a small HTTP server, and server-rendered HTML and CSS. The pantry's rules, like the 30-day window, are stored as settings an administrator can change without touching code.

Next steps

What I'd do in the next round

  • A full WCAG 2.1 AA pass: programmatic labels on every field, keyboard-only testing, and contrast checks on status colors.
  • A phone layout for the distribution floor, where staff are standing at a table rather than sitting at a desk.
  • Expiration alerts that move items close to their date to the top of the distribution list.
  • A formal usability study with timed tasks and the System Usability Scale, to measure changes between versions.
